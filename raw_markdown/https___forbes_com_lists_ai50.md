Artificial intelligence has become part of our lives, increasingly core to how we work, search for information and express ideas. In the last year, the startups spearheading this paradigm shift have raised gobs of money from venture firms to build applications used by hundreds of millions of people across professions like law, software engineering, banking and even music. Three years into the AI frenzy, startups are starting to prove they can turn lofty ideas into sustainable businesses. That’s evident in _Forbes_’ eighth annual AI 50 list, which spotlights the most promising privately-held AI companies in the world.

Juggernauts like OpenAI and Anthropic continue to be the largest companies on the list, attracting unprecedented sums of cash from marquee Silicon Valley venture capitalists and tech behemoths alike as they reportedly head towards blockbuster IPOs. The two AI giants have accumulated a combined $242.6 billion in venture funding, about 80 percent of the total $305.6 billion that the companies on this year’s AI 50 list have raised. Massive adoption of their tools has led to strong revenue growth: At the end of February, OpenAI reportedly had more than $25 billion in annualized revenue and in early April Anthropic said its revenue run rate had crossed $30 billion. And with products like Anthropic’s Claude Code and OpenAI’s Codex, the AI labs are dominating into markets like coding where players like [Cursor](https://www.forbes.com/sites/annatong/2026/03/05/cursor-goes-to-war-for-ai-coding-dominance/) (valued at $29.3 billion) must innovate to compete.

They’re not the only ones training models: San Francisco-based Physical Intelligence has raised $1 billion in funding to train foundational models for robots by collecting data from human teleoperators that control humanoids in realistic environments like kitchens and bedrooms. French startup Mistral is selling its open-weight models not just to large corporations like Cisco but also to European government agencies, for whom its local roots are a winning differentiator. (Read our feature on Mistral [here](https://www.forbes.com/sites/iainmartin/2026/04/16/how-frances-mistral-built-a-14-billion-ai-empire-by-not-being-american/).) Cambridge-based AI startup Suno is coming for an entire creative field with its music generation models.

Newcomer $8 billion-valued Reflection is also building open-source models to compete with Chinese companies like DeepSeek. It’s one of 20 total newcomers, including $2.1 billion-valued AI presentation builder Gamma, which crossed $100 million in annualized revenue with 50 employees; two-year old $1.3 billion startup Chai Discovery that’s using AI to create new medicines and speed up drug development; and New York-based Rogo, whose AI software is used by some 25,000 bankers and investors to crunch numbers.

Former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati’s Thinking Machine Labs ($2 billion raised) and legendary Stanford computer science professor Fei-Fei Li’s World Labs (focusing on spatial intelligence with more than $1 billion in funding) are among four female-led companies on the list. Others include $4 billion-valued Fireworks AI, which helps developers get access to the latest and greatest models without worrying about the technical backend.

The AI landscape has dramatically evolved over the last 12 months. Three startups on the 2025 edition of the AI 50 list were acquired or acquihired by tech giants: data labelling startup Scale AI’s CEO and cofounder Alexandr Wang left to start Meta’s superintelligence lab (The company says it remains an independent business and claims that it's seen strong revenue growth after Wang's departure.); Elon Musk’s AI startup xAI was [scooped up by SpaceX](https://www.forbes.com/sites/mattdurot/2026/02/03/elon-musk-just-became-the-first-person-ever-worth-800-billion-after-spacex-acquired-xai/) creating a combined entity worth $1.25 trillion; and Google paid $2.4 billion to hire AI coding startup Windsurf’s cofounders and license its technology. $10 billion-valued coding agent startup Cognition, which made its debut on the AI 50 list this year, [acquired the rest of Windsurf](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rashishrivastava/2025/07/15/the-prompt-windsurf-acquisition-is-the-latest-salvo-in-the-battle-for-ai-talent/).

Despite those consolidations, this year was among the most competitive for the AI 50 list. _Forbes_ received hundreds of applications. Applicants do not pay a fee to be considered and are judged for their business promise, technical talent and use of AI through a quantitative algorithm and qualitative judging panels. Companies are encouraged to share data on diversity, and our list aims to promote a more equitable startup ecosystem. This year, Forbes launched its first ever AI 50 [Brink list](https://www.forbes.com/sites/sofiachierchio/2026/04/16/the-ai-50-brink-list/) to highlight 20 up-and-coming early stage AI startups. For more, see our full package of coverage, including a detailed explanation of the list [methodology](https://www.forbes.com/sites/elisabethbrier/2026/04/16/how-forbes-compiled-the-2026-ai-50-list/), videos and analyses on trends in AI.

#### Anthropic

- CEO:DARIO AMODEI
- HQ:SAN FRANCISCO
- FUNDING:$60 BIL
- FOUNDED:2021

Anthropic had a busy February. First Claude Cowork, designed to automate common business processes, spooked the market, sending SaaS, IT and legal services shares $285 billion lower. Then it launched its latest version of Claude Opus, its coding model that’s upending software engineering. Finally, it had a very public feud with the U.S. government. After refusing to budge on safeguards around autonomous weapons and mass domestic surveillance, Anthropic was blacklisted by the Pentagon. But that’s made CEO and cofounder Dario Amodei a hero of sorts in the AI world, with rivals like OpenAI and Google coming to his defense. And business keeps booming. Revenue hit $4.5 billion last year and in February Claude surpassed ChatGPT to become the most downloaded app on the App Store. It’s now valued at $380 billion.

#### EliseAI

- CEO:MINNA SONG
- HQ:NEW YORK
- FUNDING:$392 MIL
- FOUNDED:2017

Over 80 percent of the country’s largest property management firms use EliseAI’s chatbots to speed up repetitive admin work like tracking maintenance requests, scheduling apartment tours and renewing leases. CEO Minna Song took a front desk job at a real estate firm to learn about the industry’s pain points before starting the New York-based EliseAI in 2017. At the time, she struggled to convince investors to take a chance on an AI company targeting housing. “Now we go into investor meetings and they're pitching us back to us,” says Song, 35. In 2023, the startup expanded into another crucial industry: healthcare, where its tech helps verify insurance, make appointments and answer billing questions.

#### Gamma

- CEO:GRANT LEE
- HQ:SAN FRANCISCO
- FUNDING:$91 MIL
- FOUNDED:2020

Former investment banker Grant Lee used to waste hours formatting text and designing graphics for PowerPoint presentations. Today, his startup Gamma lets anyone type in a simple text prompt to spin up sleek financial slides, visuals to go with social media posts and even entire websites. The app’s first version, launched in 2020, was a dud: one investor called it “the worst idea” they’d ever heard, Lee, 42, recalls. But the AI craze changed Gamma’s fortunes: It’s been profitable since 2023 and is now valued at $2.1 billion. One hundred million people have used its tools at least once and more than 600,000 are regular users, paying between $9 to $100 a month.

#### Reflection

- CEO:MISHA LASKIN
- HQ:BROOKLYN
- FUNDING:$2.1 BIL
- FOUNDED:2024

Chinese AI companies like DeepSeek lead the world on open-source AI models, where the underlying code is visible to clients and can be modified, which has made the Trump administration eager to champion an American alternative. Enter Reflection, a two-year-old Brooklyn-based startup that’s raised $2.1 billion and is valued at $8 billion. Backed by Nvidia, Sequoia, Lightspeed and 1789 Capital, where Donald Trump Jr. is a partner, the company hasn’t publicly released its own AI yet but is spending billions building a data center in South Korea and developing bespoke AIs for Korean companies. Former Google DeepMind researchers Misha Laskin and Ioannis Antonoglou, who led early efforts on Gemini, founded the venture in 2024.
